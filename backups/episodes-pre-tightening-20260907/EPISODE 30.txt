# Episode 30: **What Iven Copied**

## Iven Sahl

Iven Sahl woke before the bell and kept his eyes closed until the room proved it would not punish him for noticing.

Old habit.

It had survived rescue, medical clearance, several nights in the east recovery wing, one formal ruling by the Foundation Council, and Maelin's blunt opinion that fear did not become obedience just because someone had removed the person giving orders. Habits were not soldiers. They did not disband when the war moved elsewhere.

The ceiling above his bed was plain white plaster.

Noctis ceilings had been higher. Darker. Meant to make students feel watched by architecture. Aurelis ceilings were lower in the recovery rooms, and the first morning Iven had hated that because low ceilings felt like traps.

Now he thought they might only be roofs.

Hard enough.

He opened his eyes.

Morning had entered through the curtains without asking permission. It lay across the foot of his bed in a pale rectangle, touching one folded blanket, one borrowed academy sweater, and the pair of boots someone had repaired while he slept. The left boot had a new lace. The right boot still bore a tiny scorch mark near the heel from the Wounded City. No one had polished it away.

Good.

He sat up slowly. Nothing happened. Still information.

Across the room, Oric slept with his face turned into his pillow and one arm hanging off the side of the bed. Nera's cot was empty. She had started taking breakfast in the infirmary common room because Maelin had told her she could leave if the room became too quiet, and Nera had apparently decided to test the offer every morning before anyone could revoke it. On the chair beside Iven's bed, someone had left a small paper packet tied with kitchen string.

He recognized the fold. Not the handwriting. The fold.

Boot sweets.

For a while, he looked at them and did not touch.

He had sent sweets to Sera after the extraction, not because sweets fixed anything, and not because he wanted gratitude. He had sent them because there were kinds of loneliness that did not need a speech. They needed proof that someone had remembered the exact small shape of hunger.

He had written, **just so she knows someone remembered**, and then asked Mira to carry the packet because asking directly would have turned remembrance into pressure.

Now a packet sat on his own chair.

There was a note beneath the string.

Iven lifted it with two fingers.

**SERA SAID YOU CAN HAVE TWO. SHE KEPT THREE. THIS IS NOT A TRADE.**

Under that, in smaller writing:

**MIRA WROTE THIS BECAUSE SERA SAID THE WORDS AND THEN LOOKED ANGRY AT THE PENCIL.**

Iven stared at the note until the letters blurred.

Then he laughed once.

It came out badly. More breath than sound. Oric stirred and muttered something into the pillow that might have been an insult or a prayer. Iven covered his mouth.

The packet remained where it was. Not a trade.

The distinction mattered.

Noctis had taught him that gifts were advance payments. Warmth created debt. Food proved ownership. A blanket meant someone had purchased the right to ask why you were shivering. If you accepted anything, you became easier to move.

Aurelis kept failing to behave correctly.

Iven took one sweet from the packet.

Then another. Two.

The number made acceptance smaller, which made it possible.

He placed the rest back on the chair, retied the string badly, and looked at his wrist.

The Green Circle answered in a soft ring beneath his skin.

Green.

Foundation, the primers said. Sixty percent. Common.

Necessary maintenance and base function.

He had copied that definition so many times at Noctis that the words still sat in his hand even when he did not want them. Foundation. Maintenance. Base function. The official texts had never written **unimportant**. They had not needed to. Noctis had preferred cruelty with clean margins.

But Mira Castellan was Green Circle.

Finn Castellan was Green Circle.

The first student who copied Mira's courtyard note with an inverted letter had been Green Circle.

The kitchen apprentice who hid extra rolls in folded napkins for transfer students who still flinched at serving trays was Green Circle.

The repair aide who had fixed Iven's boot lace and left the scorch mark alone was Green Circle.

The whole academy seemed to lean on Green Circle hands when the beautiful parts of the world broke.

Maybe the definition had always been wrong.

Or maybe the definition had been honest in a language Noctis did not respect.

Foundation.

The part that held.

Iven pressed his thumb lightly to the ring until the glow warmed against his skin.

It did not become brighter.

It did not need to.

The morning bell rang badly from the west tower. The cracked note wavered, recovered, and joined the others late.

Oric lifted his head just enough to glare at the window.

Oric: "Bell has structural problems."

Iven: "Halden called it brave."

Oric considered that with the grave hatred of someone still half asleep.

Oric: "Professor Halden should apologize to architecture."

Iven smiled before he could stop himself.

The expression felt unfamiliar on his face. Not wrong. Just newly issued.

He dressed quietly, put Sera's note inside the inner pocket of his borrowed sweater, and left the two remaining sweets where Oric would see them when he woke properly. He did not write a note. Not every kind thing needed instructions.

---

Mira's archive station had moved twice since the ruling.

Mira kept winning arguments with furniture.

The first station, near the infirmary hall, had become too crowded after the Foundation Council posted the decision. Students came to read witness summaries, ask whether their copied statements mattered, and stare at the ugly copies as if imperfection might become contagious if they stood too close.

The second station, under the east arcade, had failed because rain blew sideways through the arches and Mira had declared that water damage was not a valid form of public discourse.

The third station occupied a wide table in the old bell tower courtyard, close enough to the public boards to see new postings, far enough that no one could pretend they had joined a witness process by hovering with dramatic concern. Two Green Circle aides sorted paper into stacks. A Blue Circle student sharpened pencils with unnecessary intensity. Aira Solen sat on the table's far end, cross-legged and writing three things at once.

Mira stood at the center of it all with ink on her fingers and tape on her sleeve.

She looked up when Iven approached. Not quickly. Not with surprise.

Like she had left room in the morning for him to arrive and was now using it.

Mira: "Iven."

His name did not sound like assignment when she said it.

The open door helped.

Iven: "You said I could ask questions."

Mira: "Yes."

He waited for the condition.

None came.

Aira glanced over the top of her page.

Aira: "Careful. She means that literally. I asked one question about filing categories and lost forty-seven minutes to moral infrastructure."

Mira: "You took notes."

Aira: "Under duress."

Mira: "You asked follow-up questions."

Aira: "Under intellectual duress."

Iven almost laughed again. He was beginning to worry it might become a medical symptom.

Mira pulled a chair out with her foot.

Mira: "Sit if you want."

If you want.

A small door instead of a hand on his back.

Iven sat.

For a moment he only looked at the table. Papers lay everywhere, but not randomly. One stack held formal witness statements with Council seals. Another held ugly copies. Another held correction records where students had copied hostile edits exactly, mistakes included, so Noctis could not later pretend the ugliness had been invented by Aurelis. In the center of the table lay Cael's final copied line under a clear protective sheet.

**I WANTED TO LIVE. I CHOSE ANYWAY. BOTH ARE TRUE.**

The sentence looked different in every copy.

Some letters leaned. Some lines broke early. One student had written **BOTH ARE TRUE** so hard the pencil had torn the paper. Another had misspelled **ANYWAY**, corrected it, and left the correction mark like a scar that did not need healing.

Iven touched the edge of the table, not the papers.

Iven: "Cael wrote your name on his list."

Mira's face changed very little.

Iven trusted that about her. Mira did not make grief large to prove she had received it.

Mira: "Aira told me."

Aira's pencil slowed.

Iven: "He wrote, 'Ask Mira how ugly copies work.'"

Mira looked at the protective sheet.

Mira: "Then we should answer him."

She took one blank page and placed it between them. Then she wrote a sentence across the top in clean block letters:

**A RECORD HELD BY ONE HAND CAN BE TAKEN.**

She turned the page toward Iven.

Mira: "Copy it."

Iven's fingers tightened around the pencil.

At Noctis, copying had been a test of obedience. The point was not the sentence. The point was whether your hand could disappear into the hand that wrote before you. Every difference became evidence. Every evidence became correction. Every correction became proof that someone should have corrected you sooner.

Mira waited.

She did not say it was easy.

The patience helped too.

Iven copied the sentence.

His first line came out too close to hers. Careful. Imitative. The pencil moved with old fear, matching the shape of authority because that was what his hand knew how to survive.

Then he reached **HELD** and the L tilted too far right.

He stopped.

Mira: "Keep going."

Iven: "It's wrong."

Mira: "Yes."

He looked at her.

She was not smiling.

Mira: "Keep going."

So he did.

The second half came out worse because now he knew it was wrong and had to keep writing anyway. The space between **ONE** and **HAND** was too wide. **TAKEN** looked angry. He set the pencil down as if it might accuse him.

Mira took his copy, placed a second blank page beside it, and copied his version exactly until she reached the tilted L.

Then she tilted it farther.

On purpose. Iven stared.

Mira finished the line, copied his too-wide space, made her own error in **CAN**, and set the page down.

Mira: "Now mine contains yours, and something mine added."

She slid the page to Aira without looking.

Aira accepted it with the solemnity of a priest receiving contraband and copied Mira's copy. Her handwriting was fast, sharp, and impatient with straight lines. She preserved the tilted L, exaggerated the space, kept Mira's damaged **CAN**, and accidentally dropped the final period.

Aira: "I regret nothing except punctuation."

Mira gave the third copy to the Blue Circle pencil sharpener, whose name Iven thought was Talen. Talen copied all three errors and added one of his own, making **RECORD** look like **REC0RD** because the O closed too hard.

By the fourth page, the sentence had become a small procession. Not ruined. Traveled.

Mira laid the pages in order.

Mira: "A perfect copy can be attacked as forgery. Authority likes perfect copies. They look controlled. They look official. They look as if one hand wrote all of them and many hands only pretended to exist."

She touched the first page.

Mira: "But this is my hand."

The second.

Mira: "This is yours."

The third.

Mira: "Mine again, carrying yours badly."

The fourth.

Aira: "My tragic punctuation era."

The fifth.

Mira: "Talen's pressure mark."

Talen, who had been trying very hard to become invisible, turned red.

Mira: "Each difference proves another person touched the sentence. Noctis can dispute the first record. They can claim the original was altered, forged, coerced, or taken from context. They have done all four since breakfast."

Aira: "With impressive lack of creativity."

Mira: "But when a sentence travels through many imperfect hands, revoking it requires revoking each hand separately."

Iven looked at the copies.

Iven: "And they cannot."

Mira: "Not anymore."

The words should have felt triumphant.

They did not.

They felt heavy.

Not bad heavy. Real heavy. The weight of a table that stayed where someone put it. The weight of a cup with a crooked label. The weight of a boot sweet accepted without becoming a contract.

Iven: "What if the error changes the meaning?"

Mira considered the question like it deserved more than reassurance.

Mira: "Then the new meaning belongs to the person who made it. We can mark difference. We can compare versions. We can preserve the original beside the copies. But we do not erase the hand to protect the sentence."

Iven thought about the primers.

Foundation. Maintenance. Base function.

He thought about how many times he had copied those words until they became a smaller cage than the room.

Iven: "Can I copy something else?"

Mira placed a blank page in front of him.

Mira: "Yes."

Not what. Not why. Just yes.

Iven took Sera's note from his pocket.

He did not copy all of it. Some parts belonged to Sera, and some parts belonged to the angry look she had given a pencil. But one line kept resting in his mind as if it had been waiting for a page.

He wrote:

**THIS IS NOT A TRADE.**

The T in **THIS** leaned left. He corrected it without erasing. The correction made the letter darker than the others. He almost started over.

Then he remembered Mira's voice.

Keep going.

He finished the line.

Mira did not touch the page until he pushed it toward her.

Mira: "Do you want it copied?"

The question stopped him. Want. Not should. Not may. Not must.

He looked at the sentence.

It was not elegant. It was not official. It had no seal, no witness number, no place in the Council archive unless someone argued very hard and became annoying enough to win.

But it was a sentence he had needed in his own hand.

Iven: "One copy."

Mira nodded.

She copied it beneath his version, preserving the corrected T.

Then she made her own difference. The period at the end became slightly too dark because her pencil paused there. Not a trade.

Held by two hands.

Aira looked at the page and said nothing.

Iven knew from the silence that she understood.

---

He met Cael near the recovery corridor because grief had poor timing and the academy had too many corners.

For one impossible breath, Iven's mind forgot the funeral, the board, the letter, the Foundation ruling, the black ash-light that had tried and failed to eat Cael's final words. He saw Cael ahead of him in the corridor with the morning at his shoulder, pale uniform loose over a body still learning how much space it could take, and thought:

There.

Then the truth corrected him. Not Cael. Leon.

Leon had stopped outside Cael's old room with one hand on the doorframe. The resemblance was not in his face. It was in the way grief made him stand beside things without entering them. Beside the room. Beside the chair. Beside the life that had ended too soon for apology to become practice.

Iven should have turned back.

Instead, Leon looked over.

His eyes fell to the folded page in Iven's hand.

Leon: "Archive?"

Iven nodded.

Leon: "Mira still terrifying?"

Iven: "Precisely."

Leon's mouth moved like it wanted to smile but had forgotten the sequence.

The corridor held them awkwardly.

Iven had practiced three things to say to Cael before remembering that Cael was not available for conversations anymore. The words had nowhere to go, so they kept knocking around inside him.

One of them escaped toward Leon instead.

Iven: "The charm found someone."

Leon looked at him.

Iven: "Cael's charm."

Leon's face changed. Not much. Enough.

Leon: "He gave it away."

Iven: "I guessed he would."

Leon: "How?"

Iven answered before he could make the sentence impressive.

Iven: "He held it three days before passing it on. Something you hold for three days and then give away is already practice. He was rehearsing generosity."

Leon looked down at his own hands.

For a moment Iven saw the old Blue Circle student who had once treated status like armor because no one had taught him what else hands were for. Then Leon flexed his fingers, slowly, as if checking whether the armor had loosened enough to become skin.

Leon: "Cael had honest hands."

Iven: "Yes."

Leon: "Noctis tried to make that inconvenient."

Iven: "It failed."

Leon gave a small, broken breath.

Leon: "Good."

They stood there a little longer. The room behind Leon remained half-open. Someone had placed a slate stone on the desk, ordinary and smooth, holding down a copied page. The stone did not know it was important.

Its ignorance made it better at being important.

Leon glanced at Iven's folded page.

Leon: "What did you copy?"

Iven opened it.

Leon read both lines.

**THIS IS NOT A TRADE.**

**THIS IS NOT A TRADE.**

The second version carried Mira's dark period.

Leon swallowed.

Leon: "Useful sentence."

Iven: "I think so."

Leon: "Can I..."

He stopped before the request became too large.

The restraint was new enough that Iven noticed.

Iven held out the page.

Leon took a blank slip from the desk. He copied Iven's sentence from Mira's copy, not from the original. His handwriting was cleaner than both, disciplined by years of expensive instruction, but the final word bent slightly downward.

Trade.

Like it had become tired at the end.

Leon stared at it.

Leon: "I made it worse."

Iven: "Yes."

Leon looked up.

Iven: "That means it's yours."

Leon gave the page back with the careful expression of someone being trusted with something small enough to survive.

Iven continued down the corridor.

He did not look back until he reached the corner.

Leon had placed his copy under the slate stone beside Cael's.

The stone held both.

---

Iven's first ordinary class lasted eleven minutes.

Instructor Mora taught foundational ward repair in a room with wide windows and no restraint marks. She explained every tool before passing it down the rows. Students were permitted to choose copper or chalk. The door remained open.

Iven noticed all of that.

Then she rang a brass bell to begin the exercise.

His body left the room before his mind agreed.

Desk struck knee. Chalk shattered under his shoe. Someone said his name. The corridor arrived around him with his back against stone and both hands protecting the channel points at his throat.

Noctis bells meant stand straight, hands visible, wait for correction.

This bell had meant begin.

His lungs did not care.

Instructor Mora stopped inside the doorway.

Not closer.

Instructor Mora: "The hall is available. The class is available. Neither choice changes your attendance mark today."

Iven stared at her.

Iven: "That is special treatment."

Instructor Mora: "Yes. Injury often requires it."

No apology. No lie that everyone was being treated identically.

Behind her, twenty students tried not to stare and failed in different directions.

Iven looked at the broken chalk near his boot.

Iven: "If I leave, do I fail?"

Instructor Mora: "No."

Iven: "If I return?"

Instructor Mora: "Also no."

The absence of punishment made the choice harder.

He stayed in the corridor until his hands lowered.

Then he picked up the largest chalk fragment.

He returned because the window was wide, the door remained open, and an unfinished ward on his desk had one broken line he knew how to repair.

No one applauded.

Instructor Mora did not ring the bell again.

Iven completed one corner of the ward, left the other three unfinished, and wrote beneath it:

**BELL RESPONSE INTERRUPTED WORK. WORK RESUMED.**

Instructor Mora marked the repaired corner correct.

She marked the unfinished sections tomorrow.

A Blue first-year raised his hand before Iven left.

Blue Student: "Should we stop using the bell?"

The whole class looked at Instructor Mora.

She looked at Iven.

Not forcing him to decide school policy. Asking whether he had an immediate condition.

Iven considered the brass bell on her desk.

Removing it forever would make the room quieter. It would also let Noctis keep deciding what the sound meant.

Iven: "Say before you ring it. Not today. Next class, once."

Instructor Mora wrote the condition on the board.

**BELL ANNOUNCED. ONE RING. EXIT REMAINS OPEN.**

The Blue student copied it into his notes.

Iven left the chalk fragment on his desk for tomorrow.

As he reached the corridor, Instructor Mora removed the bell from the center of her desk and placed it near the open door.

Not hidden.

Not centered.

The Blue student adjusted his seat so Iven would have a clear path outside next class. Another student moved the chalk tray within reach without looking at him for thanks.

Small changes. Visible. Reversible.

No one announced the class had become kinder.

Iven trusted the room slightly more because it had changed shape without demanding gratitude for the effort.

That was enough for one morning.

For the first time, tomorrow looked less like a threat than a place his chalk might still be waiting.

---

Lunch in the courtyard had become complicated by freedom.

At Noctis, meals were simple because everything was assigned. Students sat by rank, offense, recovery status, and usefulness. Food arrived in portions designed to prove that someone else had measured your need correctly. Talking too much meant arrogance. Talking too little meant concealment. Dropping a utensil could become a moral failure if the right adult needed an example.

Aurelis did not understand the efficiency of cruelty.

Students sat in changing groups. Some ate on benches. Some sat on low walls. Two Blue Circle girls argued over herb bread while a Red Circle boy balanced three bowls for friends who had not arrived yet. A kitchen aide crossed the courtyard with extra fruit and let people take from the basket without writing names down.

The disorder made Iven nervous.

It also made him hungry.

He took a tray because Maelin had once said, "Eat before your body files a formal complaint," and Iven had no wish to discover what body paperwork felt like. He chose soup, bread, and an apple. Then he stood at the edge of the courtyard and looked for a place that did not require becoming part of a group.

Then he saw the girl.

She was younger than him. Eleven, maybe, or younger if fear had been stealing height. She sat near the north planter with her tray balanced on her knees and her shoulders arranged to make the smallest possible target. Her hair was cut blunt at her chin. Her borrowed Aurelis sweater was too large. One sleeve covered her wrist completely. No one sat beside her.

Not because Aurelis had rejected her. Because Aurelis students, having been lectured by Maelin, Mira, Rhea, and probably furniture by now, had become very cautious about not crowding Noctis transfers. The caution was kind. It was also lonely from a distance.

Iven recognized the posture before he recognized why.

Minimum space. Maximum invisibility.

Need hidden so well even hunger had to whisper.

He did not walk to her.

He walked toward a nearby patch of low wall that faced the same direction she did, close enough that she could include him if she wanted, far enough that she did not have to defend the space between them.

He sat.

In the direction of beside. Not beside.

The distinction mattered enough that he felt it in his knees.

He opened his bread.

The girl did not look at him.

Good.

They ate in silence while the courtyard kept being too alive around them. Aira passed with a stack of papers taller than good judgment. Cassian crossed after her carrying another stack and wearing the expression of a man who had agreed to help before learning the papers had opinions about wind. Near the fountain, Halden and President Valmont stood over an old map with the shared frown of adults trying not to let mystery set the schedule before lunch.

The girl watched none of it.

She ate soup in small measured bites.

After several minutes, she spoke without turning her head.

Girl: "You don't have to."

Iven swallowed before answering.

Iven: "I know."

She held her spoon very still.

Girl: "Then why are you doing it?"

The question had too many possible answers.

Because no one did it for me soon enough.

Because Mira taught me copies can travel.

Because Sera sent sweets back.

Because Cael held a charm for three days and then gave it away.

Because Foundation might mean something better than they told us.

All of that was too much to place on a girl defending soup.

Iven: "I was going to sit somewhere. This wall was here."

The girl's mouth tightened. Not quite belief. Not quite refusal. They ate.

Wind moved through the courtyard and lifted the corner of a posted copy on the public board. Someone had written Cael's last line in blue pencil this time. The color made it look almost gentle. Beneath it, in three different hands, students had copied the old phrase that had appeared after the hostile Noctis script burned out:

**WITNESS REMAINS WHERE CIRCLE FAILS.**

Iven did not understand Circle Zero.

He was relieved by that.

There were already too many things adults wanted children to understand quickly so the adults could feel less late.

The girl followed his gaze at last.

Girl: "Is that about him?"

Iven: "Cael?"

Girl: "The Black Circle."

Iven looked toward the witness cottage. Haru Aerlen was not visible. That seemed polite of him.

Iven: "Maybe partly."

Girl: "Noctis says he contaminated the ruling."

The words arrived flat, memorized, and tired.

Iven had heard that voice from his own mouth before. A sentence repeated enough times to stop sounding like belief and start sounding like weather.

Iven: "Noctis says many things."

Girl: "Aurelis says many things too."

Iven: "Yes."

She looked at him.

Her eyes were dark and sharp with suspicion that had been required too early.

Girl: "Then how do you know which ones are traps?"

Iven thought of perfect copies.

He thought of Mira's tilted L.

He thought of the corrected T in his own sentence.

Iven: "I don't always."

The girl's expression closed.

He kept going before she could file him under useless.

Iven: "But traps usually need you to answer faster than you can think."

She looked down at her soup.

Her sleeve had slipped half an inch. Not enough to show her Circle.

Iven: "You can take longer here."

Girl: "Who said?"

Iven almost said Maelin.

Then he imagined Maelin appearing from behind a pillar to threaten someone's pulse and decided against summoning that much authority.

Iven: "People keep proving it."

Girl: "People can stop."

Iven: "Yes."

The girl's spoon touched the bowl once.

Softly.

She waited for correction. None came.

Across the courtyard, the west bell made its cracked little sound because someone had opened the tower door too hard. Several students laughed. The girl flinched at the laughter, then seemed confused when nothing followed it.

Iven broke a piece of bread.

Girl: "What Circle are you?"

He held out his wrist.

Green light answered.

He did not make it brighter.

The girl stared.

Girl: "Foundation."

Iven: "Yes."

Girl: "Maintenance rank."

There it was.

The old definition, polished clean enough to hide the blade.

Iven looked at his own Green Circle and thought about all the versions of the sentence traveling through imperfect hands. He thought about the repair aide leaving a scorch mark on a boot because not everything damaged needed to be corrected. He thought about the kitchen string around Sera's sweets.

Iven: "That's one reading."

Girl: "There are others?"

He was twelve and had no authority.

The lack of authority felt important.

An official definition would have been too heavy. A perfect answer would have looked like another institution arriving to replace the first.

So he gave her the version he had, incomplete and still warm from being made.

Iven: "Foundation means the part that holds while other things fall."

The girl looked at him.

He wondered if he had said it badly.

Probably.

Mira would have marked the sentence more carefully. Aira would have made it sharper. Rhea would have made it public policy by evening and somehow still scared a committee into thanking her.

But the girl did not need a perfect record.

She needed something worth carrying.

Her covered wrist moved. Only slightly.

The sleeve drew back enough for him to see a ring of pale green light, unsteady at the edges, as if even the color had learned to make itself small.

Iven did not look too long.

Iven: "You don't have to show it."

Girl: "I know."

She said it like she was testing whether the words belonged to her.

Good.

They ate until both trays were empty.

When Iven stood, the girl tensed.

He picked up his tray.

Iven: "I'm returning this."

Girl: "I can see that."

It was the first thing she had said that sounded almost like annoyance instead of defense.

Iven accepted it as progress with teeth.

He carried his tray to the return table and did not look back until he had placed it down.

The girl was still there.

Her sleeve remained pushed back. Not far.

Enough.

---

The fourth copy of Iven's sentence reached the public board by accident.

Aira claimed accident, and because Aira lied with style but documented with frightening precision, people believed her only halfway.

The line appeared below a cluster of copied witness notes near the fountain:

**THIS IS NOT A TRADE.**

The first version had Iven's corrected T.

The second had Mira's dark period.

The third had Leon's tired final word.

The fourth, in Aira's hand, had no punctuation and somehow sounded more dangerous for it.

By midafternoon, someone had copied it onto a scrap and pinned it near the infirmary entrance. By evening, a kitchen aide had written it on the inside of the pantry door where students receiving extra food could see it without anyone announcing the kindness aloud. One of the younger transfer students copied it wrong as **THIS IS NOT A TRIAL**, and Maelin declared the error medically acceptable.

Iven saw that copy after dinner.

He stood in front of it for a long time. Not a trade. Not a trial.

The mistake had changed the meaning.

It had not ruined it.

He found Mira near the archive station, wrapping the day's public copies in brown paper against night damp.

Iven: "Someone wrote it wrong."

Mira followed his gaze.

Mira: "Yes."

Iven: "It's better that way too."

Mira's hands stilled around the string.

Mira: "Sometimes."

He waited.

She tied the bundle.

Mira: "Not all errors are freedom. Some are harm. Some are lies. Some are accidents that need correction because people deserve clarity. Ugly copies work because we keep the versions beside each other. We do not pretend difference automatically means truth."

Iven looked at the pantry door again.

Iven: "But this one can stay?"

Mira: "This one can stay."

Aira, from behind a stack of record sleeves, said:

Aira: "I give it three days before someone forms a committee around it."

Mira: "I will sabotage the committee."

Aira: "Excellent. I will take minutes."

Iven smiled.

This time the expression knew where to go.

Then a Red Circle third-year tried to make the sentence useful.

She arrived with two friends, a perfect braid, and a donation box polished so brightly it looked like it had never met a poor hand. The box had a paper sign taped to the front:

**GIFTS FOR NOCTIS RECOVERY STUDENTS**

Under that, in smaller, prettier writing:

**THIS IS NOT A TRADE**

Iven saw the line and felt his stomach forget what bread was for.

The third-year smiled at him with practiced gentleness.

Student: "You're Iven, right?"

He could have said no.

That was new information. Names at Noctis had not been optional. Here, for one dangerous second, he understood that lying about himself might be possible.

He did not.

Iven: "Yes."

Student: "I'm Dalia Renn. My family supplies winter linens to three hospitals. We thought the phrase was beautiful."

Beautiful.

The word slid over the sentence like varnish.

Mira's hands tightened around the brown paper bundle.

Aira stood behind the archive table, already alert. Her first mistake was thinking alert meant correct.

Aira: "Donation boxes require approval through the student welfare office, public ledger review, and anti-patronage language."

Dalia's smile did not move.

Dalia: "Of course. We want to do this properly."

The properly landed wrong.

Iven knew because his shoulders had already risen toward his ears.

Aira heard paperwork and drew a sword made of policy. She was good at that. Too good. In a different room, against a different threat, it would have worked.

This threat had ribbons.

Dalia turned the polished box so the copied line faced the courtyard.

Student: "We only wanted to help. Unless help is offensive now."

A few students nearby slowed.

There it was.

The trap that did not need Noctis seals because ordinary pride could carry it.

If Iven objected, he became ungrateful. If Mira objected, Green Circle witness work became jealous. If Aira objected harder, Dalia became a generous student punished by procedural suspicion.

Aira opened her mouth.

Mira stepped on her foot.

Aira made a tiny sound.

Mira did not look sorry.

Mira: "You copied it wrong."

Dalia blinked.

Student: "Pardon?"

Mira pointed at the sign.

Mira: "Your T is too clean."

Aira stared at her.

Iven did too.

Mira lifted her own copy from the bundle, the one with Iven's corrected T darkened by hesitation.

Mira: "This sentence came from a specific hand. The correction mark matters."

Dalia's smile sharpened.

Student: "We can fix the lettering."

Mira: "No."

The word surprised everyone, including Mira.

Color climbed her face. She hated that. It made her look younger, and being younger in public was one of the disadvantages adults pretended not to use.

She kept going anyway.

Mira: "You cannot fix it by making the mistake prettier. You have to ask whether he wants that sentence on your box."

Dalia looked at Iven. Finally. Not as symbol. Not as recovery category. As obstacle.

That felt worse, but cleaner.

Dalia: "Do you?"

Every answer in Iven's mouth tried to become strategy.

Noctis would have taught yes, because refusing generosity revealed arrogance.

Aurelis was teaching maybe, because people here liked questions large enough to hide in.

Sera's note had said not a trade because Sera said the words and then looked angry at the pencil.

Iven held that anger for a moment.

It helped.

Iven: "No."

Dalia's friends shifted.

Dalia's smile vanished for half a second before training found it again.

Student: "Then we will remove the phrase."

Iven looked at the polished box.

At the word gifts.

At the empty slit where coins would disappear before anyone had chosen what receiving them meant.

Iven: "And the box."

Dalia: "The box is just for collection."

Iven: "It looks like a mouth."

That was not a good argument.

It was not balanced, legal, fair, or easy to enter into minutes.

It was true.

Mira's expression changed.

Aira's did too, but hers changed into horror first because she had almost argued Dalia into winning the wrong fight beautifully.

Aira: "Iven is right."

The words came stiffly. Not because she doubted them.

Because being socially wrong in public tasted terrible and she had no prepared category for it.

Aira: "I was answering the office problem. This is not an office problem."

Mira, still flushed, muttered:

Mira: "Miracle."

Aira: "Do not enjoy this."

Mira: "I am enjoying it privately."

Aira: "You are doing it with your entire face."

The argument was petty. Small.

Useful in the way ordinary weather was useful after a storm.

Dalia looked from one of them to the other and discovered that the audience had stopped forming around her generosity. Students were watching Iven now. Not kindly. Not cruelly. Waiting. That was its own pressure.

He could feel it trying to make him wise.

He refused.

Iven: "If people want to give food, put it on the table. No names. No box. No phrase."

Dalia's jaw tightened.

Student: "Then how will people know where it came from?"

Mira's answer came too fast.

Mira: "They won't."

Dalia looked genuinely offended.

For some reason, that was the thing that made Iven less afraid.

Her generosity had wanted a witness.

His hunger did not owe it one.

Dalia took the sign off the box.

Too sharply.

The tape tore.

Aira winced at the paper damage and then clearly decided not to have that reaction where anyone could respect it.

Dalia left with her friends and her polished box.

By lunch, a plain tray appeared near the pantry door with bread, oranges, wrapped sweets, and one folded note in kitchen handwriting:

**TAKE IF USEFUL. LEAVE IF NOT.**

No one signed it.

Mira copied the note because she could not help herself.

Then she wrote, very small at the bottom of her copy:

**Better than Dalia's box.**

Iven saw it.

Mira saw him seeing it.

Mira: "That line is not for public archive."

Iven: "It's petty."

Mira: "Yes."

He waited for her to apologize.

She did not.

Mira: "Some records are mine before they are useful."

Iven thought about his corrected T.

About Cael's line under the slate stone.

About a girl at the north planter keeping her sleeve half-pushed back because no one had demanded the whole wrist.

Iven: "Good."

The plain tray lasted exactly one hour before someone tried to organize it.

This time it was not Dalia.

It was a Green Circle boy with ink on both cuffs and the desperate expression of a person who had mistaken usefulness for safety. His name was Pellar, Iven thought. Or Pellin. Something with a P that he had heard during public copy sorting and failed to keep because too many new names had arrived without warnings attached.

Pellar brought three columns drawn on scrap paper.

**FOOD LEFT**

**FOOD TAKEN**

**BALANCE**

He looked proud.

He also looked terrified.

Mira saw the columns and made a small noise of pain.

Pellar: "It's not a ledger. Not officially. I just thought if people knew supply levels, they could replace things before the tray went empty."

Aira, still recovering from being wrong about Dalia's box, visibly forced herself not to answer first.

The effort looked almost medical.

Mira: "Why balance?"

Pellar tapped the third column.

Pellar: "So nobody takes too much."

The words were ordinary.

They were also a door with old hinges.

The younger transfer girl from the north planter had come near the pantry table for an orange. She stopped with her hand halfway out. Sleeve still pushed back enough to show Green. Her fingers closed on air, then retreated to her side.

Iven saw it.

Pellar did too, and his face changed with horror.

Pellar: "I didn't mean--"

The apology rushed out too fast.

Fast apologies were sometimes another way to ask the injured person to make the room kind again.

The girl stepped back.

Iven moved before he knew what he was doing. Not toward her.

Toward the paper.

He picked it up.

Pellar flinched as if expecting punishment.

Iven held the three-column sheet and felt the old Noctis part of his hand understand it perfectly. Food left. Food taken. Balance. Proof that hunger had been measured. Proof that receiving could become debt without anyone using the word.

The paper was not evil. That was inconvenient.

Pellar had not built a trap. He had built a nervous little bridge and accidentally made it look like a gate.

Iven: "Supply is useful."

Pellar blinked.

Iven tore off the third column. Not neatly.

The rip ran through the word balance until only **bal** remained on one side and **ance** on the other. Aira made a strangled sound because she was apparently capable of mourning paper while respecting trauma.

Iven gave the torn balance column back to Pellar.

Iven: "This part is not."

Pellar stared at it.

Then at the girl.

Pellar: "I'm sorry."

This time he said it slower. Not asking her to fix his face.

The girl looked at the tray.

At the oranges. At Iven.

Girl: "Can there be a sign?"

Mira's pencil lifted.

The girl noticed and frowned.

Girl: "Don't write me down."

Mira lowered the pencil immediately.

Mira: "Sorry."

That apology was quick, but not demanding. It landed and stayed small.

The girl thought for a moment.

Girl: "A sign that says you can take one and also more than one."

Pellar looked confused.

Girl: "Because one sounds safe until you're still hungry."

Iven's chest hurt.

He did not make it visible if he could help it.

Aira reached for a blank scrap, then paused and offered it to the girl instead.

Aira: "Do you want to write it?"

The girl shook her head so sharply her hair struck her cheek.

Aira accepted the answer without argument. That was progress.

Pellar took the blank scrap.

His hand shook worse than Mira's had that morning.

Pellar: "What should it say?"

The girl looked at the tray again.

Girl: "Take what helps. More can come."

Pellar wrote it.

His lettering was too narrow, crowded at the edges by fear of using too much paper.

Mira did not correct him.

Pellar taped the sign above the tray.

**TAKE WHAT HELPS. MORE CAN COME.**

For several minutes, no one took anything.

Then the girl took two oranges.

She held them against her sweater like stolen suns and walked back toward the planter without looking at anyone.

Pellar watched her go.

Pellar: "I almost made it bad."

Iven thought about perfect copies, ugly copies, boxes, ledgers, balance columns, and all the ways rescue could become another room if people were too frightened to leave anything unmeasured.

Iven: "Almost is better than after."

Pellar looked at the torn column in his hand.

Pellar: "Can I keep this?"

Iven did not understand why anyone would want to keep a mistake.

Then he did.

Iven: "Yes."

Pellar folded the torn balance column and put it in his pocket with the care of someone carrying a private warning.

Aira exhaled.

Aira: "I hate how educational this has become."

Mira: "You love educational things."

Aira: "I love educational things when I am the one doing the educating."

Mira smiled before she could make it polite.

Mira: "That is visible."

Aira looked personally injured.

Iven took one wrapped sweet from the tray.

Then, after thinking about the sign, took a second. No one wrote it down.

The sky over Aurelis had turned violet, and the lamps along the courtyard paths lit themselves one by one. At the fountain, Halden still stood with President Valmont over the old map. Neither looked happy. That made Iven think the map was telling the truth.

The water at the fountain's center darkened briefly. Not black like Haru's Circle.

Older.

Less like color than the place color had failed to arrive.

Iven felt his Green Circle warm against his wrist.

The dark reflection formed the suggestion of a ring under the academy stones, broken at four points, with a fifth space left empty at the center.

Then it vanished. No alarm sounded. No one screamed.

Halden grabbed the edge of the fountain like a man whose scholarly joy had just tried to drag him into a cellar.

President Valmont said something Iven could not hear.

Halden nodded immediately, which meant she had either threatened him or become extremely reasonable in a more dangerous tone.

Circle Zero could wait one more night.

Iven thought that was right.

Some doors should learn patience before opening.

He returned to the recovery wing with two copies in his pocket.

One said:

**THIS IS NOT A TRADE.**

One said:

**THIS IS NOT A TRIAL.**

He did not know which one he needed more.

So he kept both.

## Coda: The Name After the Bell

The younger transfer girl found Iven near the north planter the next morning.

She carried one orange and sat with the low wall between them. Close enough to speak. Far enough that neither body became a demand.

Iven broke his bread and left half on the stone.

She placed the orange beside it.

Girl: "Not trade."

Iven: "I know."

She studied him until she believed he might.

The cracked west bell rang late. Students crossed the courtyard without arranging themselves around the two Green Circle children.

After the last note, the girl told Iven her name.

Quietly.

He repeated it once and got one sound wrong. She corrected him. He tried again.

This time she stole the smaller piece of bread.

He let her.

The fountain darkened at its center before either of them finished eating. Four breaks appeared in the reflected ring below Aurelis. A fifth space remained open.

Pale writing moved beneath the water:

**THE FIFTH TOWER REMEMBERS.**

Iven did not run for Haru.

He called Mira.

That difference was the first answer.
